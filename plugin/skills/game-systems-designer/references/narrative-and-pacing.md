# Narrative and pacing

Every game carries narrative weight, even systems-led ones. *Tetris* has narrative ("the line is rising; you survive"). *Vampire Survivors* has narrative ("you are alone against an endless horde"). Narrative design in systems-led game: **deliver theme, fantasy, stakes through systems already in game** — not bolt separate story on top.

## What narrative does in a game

- **Justifies the fantasy.** Systems give player verbs; narrative explains *why* this fantasy worth investing in.
- **Charges the stakes.** Loss in system without narrative = number going down. Loss with narrative = *something happening to character, place, world*.
- **Carries the meta loop.** Story beats are strongest meta-progression hooks (player wants to know what happens next).
- **Creates the talking point.** Stories travel. Mechanics rarely do. What players post about is usually a *moment* — narrative-shaped.

## Delivery channels

Pick channels deliberately, in design-priority order:

1. **Environmental** — world and objects tell story (level design, art direction, ambient audio, found notes, environmental destruction). Cheapest per beat for systems-led games. Strongest for Discovery aesthetic.
2. **Barks** — short character lines, voice or text, event-triggered. Cheap, high reusability. Strong for Fellowship and Sensation.
3. **Item descriptions** — flavor text on cards, weapons, locations. Cheap, optional engagement. Strong for Discovery and Expression.
4. **Cutscenes / set pieces** — authored moments. Expensive per beat. Strong for Narrative aesthetic, rarely right primary channel for systems-led games.
5. **Dialogue trees / branching conversations** — high cost per beat, high engagement. Strong for Narrative + Expression (player choice). Best when verb itself is *Persuade* or *Read*.
6. **Live-ops events** — story arcs playing out across weeks of operation. High coordination cost, low cost per beat. Strong for Fellowship.
7. **Player stories** — emergent narrative players generate themselves. Lowest cost (players do the work); requires *systems producing stories worth telling*.

Most systems-led games: **environmental + barks + item descriptions + player stories**, cutscenes sparingly for climaxes.

## The minimum narrative test

Per design part, ask: *what's the minimum narrative needed for fantasy to land?*

- Hero's journey doesn't need 50 hours authored story — needs clear "before", clear "after", player carrying change in middle.
- Survival fantasy doesn't need lore dumps — needs believable threat, believable scarcity.
- Trading fantasy doesn't need named characters — needs *places feeling different from each other*, *prices responding to world*.

Add narrative until fantasy *lands*, then stop. More narrative past landing point buries systems.

## Pacing across an arc

Play arc (single playthrough, season, chapter) has same shape as single level — open / build / twist / climax / resolve — stretched out:

- **Open** — world introduces itself; verbs one at a time; stakes low
- **Build** — verbs combine; stakes rise; player understands what's at risk
- **Twist** — something unexpected reframes early game (betrayal, power swap, setting change)
- **Climax** — systems at maximum complexity; stakes highest; player has verbs and content to meet challenge
- **Resolve** — arc closes; player carries something forward (mastery, story, world-state)

Game without twist drifts. Without resolve feels truncated. Without build feels like it never started.

## Pacing within a session

Session pacing micro-cycle:

- **Tension** rises across loop (each iteration adds resources or risk)
- **Release** at end of loop (run ends, round closes, day passes)
- **Reflection** beat — moment between loops when player sees what they kept

Games skipping reflection beat (no run-summary, no end-of-day pause, no scoreboard) feel exhausting — player has no "breathing" moment.

## Pacing across days/weeks (live ops)

Live games:

- **Daily**: short engagement bursts; daily reward is *narrative beat*, not just numbers
- **Weekly**: story arc per week or two; weekly events have *names and stakes*
- **Seasonal**: story arc that resolves; next season opens new chapter
- **Annual**: major narrative reset (new continent, new era, new villain)

Live games treating narrative as decorative lose to live games using narrative as meta-progression backbone.

## Anti-patterns

- **Lore wall onboarding** — game opens with paragraphs of world history before player does anything. Cut to verb; let world reveal itself.
- **Cutscene tax** — every system unlock requires 90-second non-skippable cutscene. Players skip them; production wasted.
- **Branching that doesn't branch** — "choices" funnel back to same outcome. Narrative dynamic is *Submission*, not *Expression*.
- **Disconnected story** — cutscenes and gameplay about different things. Player learns to ignore one or other.
- **Live-ops content with no story** — events like "Event 14", no theme, characters, stakes. Nothing to talk about; engagement decays.

## Output for the design doc

In §9 of design doc:
- **Theme** in one sentence
- **Delivery channels ranked** by design priority
- **Minimum narrative for the fantasy** (cut line)
- **Player agency in story** (linear / branching / emergent / authored)
- Per arc, one-paragraph **pacing sketch** (open → build → twist → climax → resolve)
